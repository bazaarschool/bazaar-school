Nobody reads an annual report front to back, including the people who wrote it. It is assembled by five departments who never met, printed in a font chosen by the design agency, and opened by most shareholders exactly once, to find the dividend. Yet it is still the only document in which a company is legally required to tell you the truth, with an outsider's signature underneath. So the skill is not reading it. The skill is knowing where to look.

## Getting hold of one

Every listed company must publish its annual report on its own website, usually under "Investors", and file it with the exchanges, where it sits on the BSE and NSE sites under corporate filings. If you hold even one share, it arrives by email at least three weeks before the annual general meeting. Download the PDF rather than reading the web version; you will want to search it.

Use the consolidated statements, not the standalone ones, whenever a company has subsidiaries. Standalone numbers can look spotless while the mess lives in a subsidiary.

## What is inside, in one paragraph

The front half is prose: a letter from the chairman, the directors' report, a section called management discussion and analysis (MD&A), and a corporate governance report listing who sits on the board and how often they turned up. The back half is numbers: the auditor's report, the balance sheet, the profit and loss statement, the cash flow statement, and then fifty to a hundred pages of notes explaining every line. The notes are where the information is. The letter is where the adjectives are.

## Read it backwards

Start at the auditor's report. It is two or three pages near the beginning of the financial section and it has one job: to say whether an independent accountant believes the numbers. The word you want is "unmodified" or "unqualified", meaning no reservations. A "qualified" opinion means the auditor disagreed with something specific, and the report says what. An "adverse" opinion or a "disclaimer of opinion" means stop reading and sell, or at least ask why you still own it. Since 2018 the report also lists "key audit matters", the areas the auditor found hardest to verify. Read those. They are a free map of where the judgment calls were made.

Next, the cash flow statement, specifically the line "net cash from operating activities". Compare it with the net profit on the P&L, and do it for five years, not one. Profit is an opinion built from accounting choices. Cash is what arrived in the bank. In a healthy business the two track each other over time. When profit rises year after year while operating cash flow stays flat or negative, somebody is booking sales that are not being paid for, or the company is stuffing inventory, or worse.

Then the balance sheet, with three questions. How much debt is there compared with equity, and did it grow faster than the business? Did receivables (money customers owe) grow faster than sales? Did inventory? Each of those is a company lending to its customers or to its own warehouse to make the sales line look better.

Then three notes in particular. The related party transactions note lists every deal between the company and its promoters, directors and their other companies. Some are normal. A pattern of loans to promoter entities, or purchases from a supplier owned by the chairman's brother, is not. The contingent liabilities note lists claims, tax demands and guarantees that are not on the balance sheet yet. Compare the total with the company's profit; if a pending tax demand is three years of earnings, you should know. The borrowings note tells you who the lenders are and when the money is due.

Only then go back to the front and read the MD&A, which is the management's own explanation of the year, and finally the chairman's letter. Read them last because by then you can tell when they are talking around something.

## The six pages, as a checklist

If you have twenty minutes, these are the pages: the auditor's opinion; operating cash flow against net profit for five years; the balance sheet's debt, receivables and inventory against sales; the related party note; the contingent liabilities note; and the shareholding pattern, where you look for the promoter's holding and whether any of it is pledged to lenders. Rising pledges mean the promoter needed money badly enough to risk losing control of the company.

## What was sitting in Satyam's reports

On 7 January 2009 B. Ramalinga Raju, the chairman of Satyam Computer Services, wrote to the board that the company's balance sheet carried roughly 5,000 crore rupees of cash and bank balances that did not exist. The profits had been inflated for years, and the cash was invented to match. The auditors had signed every report.

The reason this story belongs in a lesson on reading, rather than one on fraud, is what the reports showed to anyone who did the arithmetic. Satyam supposedly held thousands of crores in bank deposits and earned a strangely small amount of interest on them. Its operating cash flow and its profits told different stories. A reader with the checklist above would not have proved a fraud, but would have had a question nobody answered, and an unanswered question is a reason not to own something.

## Other things that should make you sit up

An auditor resigning in the middle of the year, especially a large firm, which rarely walks away from fees without a reason. A new chief financial officer every eighteen months. "Other income" making up a growing share of profit; a software company that earns its profit from treasury gains is not a software company. Capital work in progress that never becomes a working asset. A sudden switch of auditor to a firm you have never heard of. None of these is proof of anything. Each is a reason to read the notes a second time.

## How long it takes

The first annual report you read properly will take ninety minutes and feel like homework. The second one from the same company takes thirty, because you are only looking for what changed. ## Try this before moving on

Download the latest annual report for one of the three companies you picked in the Basics course. Find the auditor's opinion and write down the single word that describes it. Then find net profit and net cash from operating activities for the last five years and put them side by side. Which is bigger, and is the gap growing? That one table tells you more than the chairman's letter will.
