An index is a thermometer. It tells you the temperature of a room full of companies without telling you anything about any single one of them. You cannot buy a thermometer reading, and you cannot buy the Sensex. But you can learn to read it, and most people who talk about it every day cannot.

## Two baskets

The Sensex is a basket of 30 large companies listed on the BSE. The exchange started publishing it in 1986 and set its value in the financial year 1978-79 at 100. So when the Sensex stands at 80,000, the basket is worth 800 times what a similar basket was worth in 1979, before counting dividends.

The Nifty 50 is the NSE's basket of 50 large companies. Its base date is 3 November 1995, at 1,000. Nifty at 25,000 means the basket has multiplied 25 times since then.

The two baskets overlap heavily. Almost every Sensex company is also in the Nifty, so on any given day they move in the same direction by nearly the same percentage. News channels show both because the exchanges are rivals, not because they disagree.

## Points are not percentages

This is the single most useful habit in the lesson. Whenever you hear a number of points, divide by the index level.

A 400 point fall when the Sensex is at 80,000 is half a percent. The same 400 points in 2006, when the Sensex first crossed 10,000, was four percent, a genuinely bad day. Headlines love "biggest point fall in history" because the index is high, so the point moves are large even when nothing dramatic has happened. On 23 March 2020 the Sensex lost 3,935 points. That was frightening because it was 13 percent, not because it was 3,935.

Make it a reflex: points divided by level. You will stop being scared by the wrong things.

## How the basket is weighted

Each company's influence on the index depends on its free-float market capitalisation. Market cap you met in the first chapter: price times shares. Free float means only the shares actually available to trade are counted, so a promoter's 60 percent holding is left out.

The effect is that the index is top-heavy. A handful of names, a few big banks, Reliance Industries and the large IT companies, together carry a large slice of the weight. On a day when the index falls one percent, it is common to find that two or three heavyweights did most of the damage while thirty smaller constituents barely moved. "The market fell" often means "HDFC Bank and Reliance fell".

## Companies come and go

The baskets are reviewed twice a year. A company that shrinks, gets taken over or loses liquidity drops out, and a rising one takes its place. Satyam left the Sensex in days after its accounting fraud became public in January 2009. Zomato, a company that did not exist as a listed share before 2021, entered the Sensex in December 2024. It has since renamed itself Eternal.

This quiet pruning matters for one reason. The long-term chart of an index looks smoother than the long-term experience of holding any thirty individual shares, because the index drops its failures and you would have been holding them.

## A few milestones, for a sense of scale

The Sensex crossed 1,000 in 1990, 10,000 in February 2006, 50,000 in January 2021 and 80,000 in July 2024. The Nifty crossed 10,000 in July 2017, 20,000 in September 2023 and 25,000 in August 2024.

Notice the gaps. Sixteen years from 1,000 to 10,000, then fifteen years from 10,000 to 50,000, then three and a half years from 50,000 to 80,000. That is what compounding looks like from the inside: the same percentage growth produces bigger and bigger absolute numbers, which is also why point-based headlines get more breathless every year.

## When the thermometer is switched off

If the Nifty 50 or Sensex moves 10 percent in a day, in either direction, trading on both exchanges halts, for 45 minutes if it happens before 1 pm and for shorter periods later in the day. At 15 percent it halts again for longer, and at 20 percent trading stops for the rest of the day. These are the market-wide circuit breakers. They were triggered twice in March 2020, on the 13th and the 23rd. They exist to force a pause, not to stop a fall.

## The other indices you will hear about

The Nifty Next 50 holds the 50 companies just below the Nifty 50, the waiting room for the big index. Nifty Midcap 150 and Nifty Smallcap 250 track the next tiers down, where prices swing more. Nifty Bank, often called Bank Nifty, tracks twelve large banks and is the favourite of derivatives traders, which is a sentence you should file away for the Advanced course. The Nifty 500 and BSE 500 cover most of the market's total value and are the closest thing to "the whole Indian market" in one number.

## You cannot buy it, but you can buy a copy

Nobody sells you a Sensex. What you can buy is an index fund or an exchange traded fund that holds the same 30 or 50 shares in the same proportions and therefore moves almost exactly like the index, minus a small fee. For many people this ends up being most of their equity money, and there is no shame in that. A later lesson covers how.

## Why a beginner should care about any of this

Because the index is your examiner. Whatever you buy over the next five years, the Nifty 50 was the free alternative you turned down. If your picks did not beat it, the hours were entertainment, not investing. That is not an argument against picking shares. It is the standard you will be marked against, and it is better to know the standard on day one.

## Try this before moving on

Find today's Nifty 50 closing level on the NSE website. Work out what a one percent move is in points and write it on a sticky note. Then open the index's list of constituents and look at the weights of the top ten. Count how much of the index those ten companies are. You will not forget the top-heaviness after you see it.
