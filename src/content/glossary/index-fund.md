An index fund does not pick shares. It holds what the index holds, in the same weights, and changes only when the index does. That keeps it cheap: the yearly fee, called the expense ratio, is often a small fraction of what actively managed funds charge.

An exchange traded fund (ETF) does the same job but trades on the stock exchange like a share, so you need a demat account to buy one. An index fund is bought from the fund house at the day's price, the NAV, often through a monthly SIP.

### Example

You put 10,000 rupees in a Nifty 50 index fund with an expense ratio of 0.2 percent. The fee is about 20 rupees a year. If the Nifty rises 12 percent, your fund rises a little less than that, because of the fee and small tracking differences.

### Watch out

Compare tracking error as well as the fee. Two funds copying the same index can drift a fraction of a percent apart every year, and that adds up.
